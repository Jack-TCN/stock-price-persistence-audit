\section{Related work}

Financial forecasting studies differ in their targets, benchmarks, and use of training data. A gain in direction classification does not establish a gain in price-level regression, and a zero-shot result does not describe every foundation model. Table~\ref{tab:related_scope} summarizes these distinctions in the work most relevant to this study. The review then connects financial targets, forecasting architectures, and model adaptation to the present audit.

\subsection{Financial targets and benchmarks}

Bao et al.\ combine wavelet processing, stacked autoencoders, and LSTM for future closing-price prediction \cite{bao2017deep}. Patel et al.\ instead study price movement using technical indicators and four classifiers \cite{patel2015trend}. Their classification target differs from the absolute price-level target here. Sezer et al.\ review deep learning for financial time-series forecasting, while Ozbayoglu et al.\ survey a wider range of financial applications \cite{sezer2020stockreview,ozbayoglu2020financialsurvey}. These studies provide context for model and feature choices, but their different tasks do not form a common persistence benchmark.

Simple benchmarks also play a central role in return prediction. Welch and Goyal document out-of-sample difficulties in equity-premium prediction relative to a historical-average benchmark \cite{welch2008comprehensive}. Campbell and Thompson show that weak restrictions can improve predictive regressions and allow many of them to beat that benchmark \cite{campbell2008predicting}. Their findings demonstrate why the evaluation design matters, rather than a universal failure of learned forecasting. Both studies concern returns, so neither directly establishes a result about future absolute closing prices.

General forecasting research provides a further reason to keep simple comparators visible. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos define the naive forecast as the latest observation and use it as a benchmark \cite{hyndman2018forecasting}. Makridakis et al.\ compare machine-learning and statistical methods on monthly competition series and emphasize broad, explicit benchmark evaluation \cite{makridakis2018concerns}. Their experiment concerns a different collection of series and methods. We adopt the evaluation principle, while deriving the stock-price conclusions from the present experiment.

\subsection{Forecasting architectures}

Deep forecasting surveys describe recurrent, convolutional, attention-based, and other model families \cite{lim2021survey,benidis2022tutorial}. Recent architectures change how models represent observations rather than changing the need for a suitable benchmark. DLinear combines moving-average decomposition with separate linear projections of the resulting components \cite{zeng2023transformers}. PatchTST uses patches and channel-independent modeling, while iTransformer represents individual variates as tokens \cite{nie2023patchtst,liu2024itransformer}. TimeMixer combines decomposition with mixing across temporal scales \cite{wang2024timemixer}.

The present audit uses these papers to motivate several architectural motifs. Its supervised implementations include recurrent models, a linear temporal proxy, and compact patch-based, variate-token, and multiscale proxies. The linear proxy omits the decomposition in DLinear, and the other proxies do not reproduce all components or settings of the corresponding published systems. Their results therefore describe these implementations under the present training protocol. They do not rank the official architectures or establish their best achievable performance.

\subsection{Foundation models and adaptation}

Chronos scales and quantizes time-series values for pre-trained probabilistic forecasting \cite{ansari2024chronos}. Lag-Llama, TimesFM, and Moirai also report zero-shot forecasting results, using different model designs and training collections \cite{rasul2023lagllama,das2024timesfm,woo2024moirai}. MOMENT covers a broader set of time-series analysis tasks and evaluates adaptation with limited supervision and task-specific fine-tuning \cite{goswami2024moment}. These models do not share one interchangeable training or adaptation protocol. Success on their benchmark tasks alone does not establish an advantage over persistence for the stock-price target here.

This study evaluates only Chronos-T5-small without asset-specific fine-tuning. Its contexts, input variables, and sampled test origins differ from those of the supervised models. We therefore report its results separately and use a small full-origin subset as a limited sensitivity check. The central question is whether each selected implementation improves on its own matched persistence baseline. The experiment does not test the other foundation models or provide a controlled ranking across model families.
